Vouch: three design directions

For the BLI Legal Tech Hackathon 2 entry. The audience is lawyers and compliance officers, so every direction starts from the tools they already trust and puts the evidence first. Each one is applied to the same real claim: Qwen 2.5 7B's "Depreciation for Q3 2026 was $1.37 billion", checked against Tesla's 10-Q for the quarter ended June 30, 2026.

The mocks use the words and transactions of the 24 September round. The line "Checked by the Chainlink CRE workflow" is how a claim will read once the testnet round runs.

Recommendation: A, The Record.

A lawyer's first question about any claim is "where does it say that?". The Record answers it the way a brief or a memo does: the claim in a serif quotation, the filing's sentence highlighted in context, numbered findings, and the provenance in a footnote. Its verdict signal borrows the citator flag (the red or green flag that tells a lawyer whether an authority is still good law), which this panel reads without a legend. It is also the direction least like any other entry in a crypto-sponsored hackathon. From B, keep one thing: the mismatched words in the claim get a red wavy underline, so the error is visible before the reader gets to the findings.

A. The Record a claim read like a page of a legal memoRecommended

Paper white, black ink, one red and one green. Quoted words (the claim, the filing) are set in Source Serif 4, as quotations are in a brief; the interface is IBM Plex Sans; hashes and accession numbers are IBM Plex Mono and live in footnotes and the margin. The verdict is a citator-style flag. The filing's matching sentence is highlighted as a reader would highlight it, with the surrounding sentence kept for context.

B. Redline a claim compared against the filing, like a document comparison

A working tool, all IBM Plex. The hero is a two-pane comparison: the claim on the left, the filing on the right, with the words that disagree struck through in red and the filing's words double-underlined in blue, the convention every lawyer knows from comparing drafts. Dense, grey, precise. Strong for the error; weaker at telling the story of what happened next.

C. Workpaper a claim tied out like an audit workpaper

Navy ink, a header band, ruled tables and tickmarks: the way an auditor ties a number in a report to its source and signs it off. Public Sans (the typeface of the US government's design system) gives it a regulator's plainness. Very credible to compliance and finance readers; the grid can feel like a form, and it says less about the AI story.

TypePublic Sans for everything set in words, Source Code Pro for references and hashes, tabular figures throughout
